\section*{The Lumber Camp}\stepcounter{section}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The Lumber Camp}
{\entryfont The characters eventually come across a small human lumber camp near the outer reaches of the Woods of Lomond. For generations, the workers here have heard stories of strange lights, elusive forest spirits, and unusual creatures deeper within the woods, but few have ever knowingly encountered the inhabitants of Ty Coeden.

The meeting provides the Caelthir with one of their first opportunities to approach ordinary people of Fife openly and on their own terms. The lumberjacks are wary and visibly unsettled by the unexpected visitors, but they are not immediately hostile. How the characters handle this first contact may shape how both groups view one another long after the encounter ends.}
\subsection*{Approaching the Camp}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Approaching the Camp}
{\entryfont The lumber camp lies in a broad clearing where several recently felled trees have been stripped of their branches and stacked beside rough timber shelters. A handful of wagons stand nearby, while draft horses wait beneath a simple lean-to. The camp is active when the characters arrive, and their approach is noticed almost immediately.

The workers are accustomed to unusual sounds and fleeting shapes within the forest, but openly approaching Fey creatures are another matter entirely. Their first reaction should be one of surprise and apprehension rather than aggression.}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	The rhythmic crack of axes carries between the trees long before the camp itself comes into view. Ahead, the forest opens into a broad clearing scattered with timber piles, rough shelters, and several heavy wagons loaded with freshly cut logs.

	One by one, the sounds of work begin to stop.

	A lumberjack lowers his axe. Another turns toward the tree line. Within moments, nearly every pair of eyes in the camp is fixed upon you.

	A few workers take cautious steps backward. Others tighten their grip upon tools that suddenly look far more like weapons.

	Finally, a broad-shouldered man near the centre of the camp raises one hand toward the others before calling out:

	"That's close enough. Who - or what - are you?"
\end{DndReadAloud}
\subsection*{First Contact}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{First Contact}
{\entryfont The foreman does not approach immediately. Instead, he keeps a cautious distance while the other workers watch from behind carts, timber piles, and half-finished shelters. Most of the lumberjacks have spent years hearing stories about strange beings deeper within the Woods of Lomond, and several are visibly unsettled to discover that at least some of those stories were true.

The characters are free to introduce themselves however they wish. A calm explanation, an open display of peaceful intent, or even a small gesture of goodwill is enough to prevent the situation from escalating. The lumberjacks are wary, but they have no desire to provoke a fight with creatures they barely understand.

Avoid resolving the entire exchange with a single Charisma check. Instead, allow the characters' words and actions to shape the workers' reactions. Ability checks may support a particular approach, but successful roleplay should be sufficient to earn a measure of trust.

Once the workers are convinced that the characters mean them no harm, their fear gradually gives way to curiosity. Questions begin cautiously at first, before becoming increasingly direct:
\begin{itemize}
	\item "Have you really been living somewhere in these woods all this time?"
	\item "Were those lights people kept seeing yours?"
	\item "Do you know what else lives deeper in there?"
	\item "Why come speak to us now?"
	\item "Are there more of you?"
\end{itemize}
\noindent The characters are under no obligation to reveal anything they believe should remain secret. How openly they answer these questions may influence the tone of the encounter, but the lumberjacks do not press aggressively if the characters choose to remain guarded.

The foreman eventually relaxes enough to lower his voice and remark:}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	"Suppose I always thought the stories were meant to keep children from wandering too far into the trees."

	He glances toward the surrounding forest.

	"Didn't expect the stories to come walking into camp and introduce themselves."
\end{DndReadAloud}
\subsection*{A Shared Problem}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A Shared Problem}
{\entryfont As the conversation begins to settle into something resembling ordinary hospitality, a sudden crack cuts through the camp. One of the timber stacks shifts unexpectedly, sending several heavy logs rolling toward a nearby wagon. A worker attempting to get clear is knocked to the ground and becomes trapped beneath part of the fallen timber.

The incident gives the characters an immediate opportunity to cooperate with the lumberjacks rather than simply speak with them. The problem should be presented as a flexible challenge with several possible solutions. Characters might lift or lever the logs aside, calm frightened draft horses, use magic to stabilize the stack, direct the workers, or find another creative way to free the trapped lumberjack.}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	A sharp crack echoes across the clearing.

	One of the stacked timber piles shifts violently before several heavy logs break loose and roll toward a nearby wagon. Workers shout and scatter as the horses rear against their harnesses.

	Then someone cries out.

	A lumberjack lies pinned beside the wagon, one leg trapped beneath a fallen log while the remaining timber above him creaks dangerously.
\end{DndReadAloud}
{\noindent\entryfont The lumberjacks immediately rush to help, but panic and the unstable timber make coordinated action difficult. Allow the players to decide how they assist before calling for checks. Straightforward solutions might involve \textbf{Strength (Athletics)} to move the timber, \textbf{Wisdom (Animal Handling)} to control the horses, or an appropriate spell or tool proficiency to stabilize the situation. Particularly effective plans may grant advantage or remove the need for a check entirely.

Failure should complicate the rescue rather than result in the worker's death. A shifting log might cause minor damage, the horses may begin to bolt, or another section of timber may become unstable. The encounter is intended to create cooperation, not punish experimentation.

Once the worker has been freed and the immediate danger has passed, the atmosphere changes noticeably. Whatever uncertainty remained about the visitors is now tempered by the simple fact that, when something went wrong, both groups acted together. The foreman thanks the characters directly, and several workers who had previously kept their distance become far more willing to speak with them.}
\subsection*{Around the Camp}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Around the Camp}
{\entryfont With the immediate danger resolved, the encounter can settle into a quieter period of conversation. The lumberjacks return to their work, though several remain nearby, eager to learn more about their unexpected visitors. Food and drink may be offered, tools are put away, and the camp gradually begins to feel less like a place preparing for trouble and more like a gathering of curious neighbours.

Use this time to let the characters learn about ordinary life beyond Ty Coeden. The workers can speak about nearby settlements, trade routes, seasonal work, local dangers, and the practical difficulties of living near the Woods of Lomond. They possess no great political insight, but their experiences provide a grounded picture of the people with whom the Caelthir may eventually share more regular contact.

The characters may also ask about the camp's logging practices. The foreman explains that the workers deliberately avoid cutting too deeply into the forest, partly because the terrain becomes increasingly difficult and partly because generations of warnings have made them reluctant to disturb the older woods. If the characters raise concerns about sacred groves, unusual plants, or other places important to the Caelthir, the lumberjacks are willing to listen and discuss boundaries rather than dismiss them outright.

This scene should remain open-ended. Encourage casual conversation, small cultural misunderstandings, shared meals, stories, or demonstrations of unfamiliar customs. The purpose is not to establish a formal agreement between Ty Coeden and the camp, but to show that meaningful contact can begin through ordinary interactions long before treaties or alliances become necessary.}
\subsection*{Parting as Neighbours}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Parting as Neighbours}
{\entryfont Eventually, the characters must continue on their way. By this point, the initial fear surrounding their arrival should have given way to cautious familiarity. The lumberjacks may still regard the Caelthir as strange and unfamiliar, but they no longer see them merely as creatures from old stories.

Before the characters leave, the foreman offers a simple assurance: if members of Ty Coeden return peacefully, they will be welcomed at the camp. He makes no promises on behalf of nearby settlements or the wider Kingdom of Fife, but suggests that future visits, trade, or cooperation need not be impossible.}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	The foreman gives the group a final, thoughtful look before extending a weathered hand.

	"You've been living beside us longer than any of us knew. Suppose that makes us neighbours, whether we planned on it or not."

	He glances back toward the camp.

	"Come peacefully, and you'll find no trouble here."
\end{DndReadAloud}
{\noindent\entryfont The encounter does not create a formal alliance, nor does it settle the question of how Ty Coeden should approach the outside world. Instead, it provides the characters with something far more immediate: proof that peaceful contact is possible.

When they eventually return to Ty Coeden, they can report that the people beyond the forest are neither the monsters feared by some nor guaranteed friends. They are simply neighbours - cautious, curious, and perhaps willing to learn.}

{\entryfont Following the council's decision, the party travels to a small human lumber camp on the eastern edge of the Woods of Lomond. The camp has operated here for many years, carefully avoiding the deeper forest where strange stories and disappearances are said to occur.

The camp consists of roughly a dozen lumberjacks, a foreman, several wagons, a few draft horses, and stacks of freshly cut timber awaiting transport. It is a practical place, built for hard work rather than comfort.}

\subsection*{Arrival}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Arrival}
{\entryfont As the players emerge from the trees, the camp immediately falls silent.}

\begin{DndReadAloud}
	Axes stop swinging.

	Conversations cease.

	Several workers instinctively reach for axes or hunting bows - not to attack, but because they genuinely do not know what they are seeing.

	Some whisper:

	\textit{"Forest spirits..."}

	Others simply stare.

	The foreman steps forward, placing himself between his workers and the strangers.

	\textit{"That's close enough."}
\end{DndReadAloud}

\subsection*{Breaking the Ice}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Breaking the Ice}
{\entryfont The players now have complete freedom in how they approach the humans.

Possible approaches include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item introducing themselves peacefully
	\item explaining who they are
	\item answering questions
	\item demonstrating harmless Fey magic
	\item offering gifts
	\item helping around the camp
\end{itemize}

Avoid using Persuasion as a single solution. Let roleplay drive the conversation, calling for checks only when appropriate.}

\subsubsection*{The Humans' Perspective}
{\entryfont The lumberjacks have heard stories their entire lives.

Among them:
\begin{itemize}
	\item strange lights deeper within the woods
	\item mysterious music at night
	\item travellers disappearing
	\item trees seemingly moving overnight
	\item beautiful figures glimpsed between the branches
\end{itemize}

Most assumed these were simply campfire tales.

Seeing actual Fey folk confirms that the stories contained at least some truth.

Interestingly, they're almost disappointed.}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	One lumberjack quietly says:

	\textit{"I thought you'd be taller."}

	Another asks:

	\textit{"So... you're not ghosts?"}
\end{DndReadAloud}

\subsection*{Learning About One Another}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Learning About One Another}
{\entryfont Rather than interrogating one another, both groups gradually become curious.

The humans ask questions such as:
\begin{itemize}
	\item "Have you always lived here?"
	\item "Why hide?"
	\item "Were you watching us?"
	\item "Did you really vanish into trees?"
\end{itemize}
Meanwhile, the players may ask about:
\begin{itemize}
	\item nearby villages
	\item trade routes
	\item the Kingdom of Fife
	\item logging practices
	\item life outside the forest
\end{itemize}
Neither side possesses complete knowledge of the other, making the exchange feel genuine.}

\subsection*{A Shared Problem}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{A Shared Problem}
{\entryfont As the conversation begins to relax, a loud crack echoes through the camp.

One of the large timber stacks collapses after a supporting log shifts.

A wagon is trapped beneath the fallen timber, and one worker is pinned by his leg.

This is not a combat encounter, but a cooperative challenge.

Possible solutions include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item lifting the logs
	\item using leverage
	\item magical assistance
	\item coordinating the workers
	\item calming frightened horses before they bolt
\end{itemize}
Reward creativity over skill checks.

The important part is that humans and Caelthir solve the problem together, not that one side rescues the other.}

\subsection*{Around the Campfire}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Around the Campfire}
{\entryfont As thanks, the foreman invites the delegation to remain for the midday meal.

This provides an opportunity for quieter roleplay.

Topics might include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Human bread compared to Fey cuisine.
	\item Stories about strange creatures in the woods.
	\item Children's curiosity about the visitors.
	\item Misunderstandings about Fey customs.
	\item Questions about music, festivals, and craftsmanship.
\end{itemize}
The atmosphere should gradually shift from suspicion to genuine interest.}

\subsection*{An Unexpected Discovery}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{An Unexpected Discovery}
{\entryfont Before the players depart, one of the lumberjacks hesitantly approaches.}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	He has been carrying something for years.

	From a small wooden box, he produces a beautifully carved wooden flute.

	\textit{"Found this near the old creek fifteen years ago."}

	\textit{"Never knew who it belonged to."}

	An elder among the party immediately recognizes the craftsmanship.

	It is unmistakably Caelthir work.

	The flute belonged to one of the first scouts who vanished decades ago while observing the camp from afar.

	The lumberjack had kept it simply because he thought it was too beautiful to throw away.

	Without realizing it, he has been preserving a small piece of Caelthir history.

	He quietly returns it.

	\textit{"Seems it ought to go home."}
\end{DndReadAloud}

\subsection*{Outcome}\stepcounter{subsection}\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Outcome}
{\entryfont No treaties have been signed.

No promises have been made.

But for the first time in living memory, the Caelthir and the people of the Woods of Lomond have spoken face to face - and parted on friendly terms.}
\begin{DndReadAloud}
	The foreman leaves them with one final remark:

	\textit{"You've been our neighbors longer than either of us knew."}

	\textit{"Suppose it's about time we started acting like it."}
\end{DndReadAloud}
